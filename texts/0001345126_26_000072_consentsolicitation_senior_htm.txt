Tax Consequences to U.S. Holders Consequences of the Proposed Amendments. If the Proposed Amendments do not become effective, a U.S. Holder will not recognize gain or loss for U.S. federal income tax purposes as a result thereof. If the Proposed Amendments become effective, the tax treatment of a U.S. Holder will depend on whether the modification to the Notes results in a “deemed” exchange of such Notes for U.S. federal income tax purposes. Generally, the modification of a debt instrument will be treated, for U.S. federal income tax purposes, as resulting in a “deemed” exchange of the debt instrument for a new debt instrument if such modification is “significant” as specially determined for U.S. federal income tax purposes. Under applicable Treasury regulations, a modification generally is “significant” if, based on all the facts and circumstances, and taking into account all modifications of the debt instrument collectively, the legal rights and obligations that are altered and the degree to which they are altered are economically significant. A specific rule in the Treasury regulations provides that a modification that adds, deletes, or alters customary accounting or financial covenants is not a “significant modification,” although the rule does not define “customary accounting or financial covenants.” Another specific rule in the Treasury regulations provides that a change in the yield of a debt instrument is not a “significant modification” if the yield of the modified instrument, taking into account the Additional PIK Payment, does not vary from the yield of the unmodified instrument, determined as of the date of the modification, by more than the greater of 25 basis points or 5% of the annual yield of the unmodified instrument. 12 The change in yield of the Notes attributable to the Additional PIK Payment will not be considered a “significant modification” of the Notes. The Company intends to take the position that, although the issue is not free from doubt, the adoption of the Proposed Amendments other than the Additional PIK Payment (the “Proposed Other Amendments”) will add, delete or alter customary accounting or financial covenants, and therefore will not constitute a “significant modification” of the Notes for U.S. federal income tax purposes. If this position is correct, a U.S. Holder would not recognize any gain or loss for U.S. federal income tax purposes as a result of the adoption of the Proposed Amendments, and such Holder should continue to have the same tax basis, holding period and accrued market discount, if any, with respect to the Notes as it had before the adoption of the Proposed Amendments. The law on this point is unclear, however, and the IRS could assert that the Proposed Other Amendments constitute a significant modification of the Notes. If the Proposed Other Amendments were treated as a significant modification of the Notes, a U.S. Holder would be treated as having exchanged its “old” Notes for “new” Notes for U.S. federal income tax purposes, which would be a taxable event unless the deemed exchange were to qualify as a “recapitalization” for U.S. federal income tax purposes, which qualification is uncertain. U.S. Holders are urged to consult their tax advisors regarding whether a deemed exchange would qualify as a recapitalization. If the deemed exchange were to qualify as a recapitalization, a U.S. Holder generally would not recognize gain or loss on the deemed exchange, except that any accrued but unpaid interest would be taxable as ordinary income to the extent not previously included in income. In such event, the U.S. Holder’s initial tax basis in the “new” Notes would be an amount equal to the U.S. Holder’s tax basis in the “old” Notes, and the U.S. Holder’s holding period in the “new” Notes would include the U.S. Holder’s holding period in the “old” Notes. The U.S. Holder’s accrued market discount with respect to the “old” Notes, if any, would carry over to the “new” Notes. If the deemed exchange were treated as a taxable transaction, a U.S. Holder who holds Notes when the Proposed Amendments become effective would recognize gain in an amount equal to the excess of the “issue price” of the “new” Notes (discussed below) over the U.S. Holder’s adjusted basis in the “old” Notes. Such gain generally would be treated as capital gain except to the extent of any accrued market discount on the “old” Notes, which would be treated as ordinary income, and any accrued but unpaid interest on the “old” Notes, which would be treated as ordinary interest income. Any loss realized may be subject to disallowance under the “wash sale” rules. A U.S. Holder's adjusted tax basis in its “old” Note generally will be an amount equal to the U.S. Holder's purchase price for the “old” Note, increased by market discount, if any, previously included in income and reduced, but not below zero, by any bond premium previously amortized, on the “old” Note. A U.S Holder’s initial tax basis in any “new” Notes would be equal to the issue price of such “new” Notes except where the wash sale rules would apply to disallow a loss, and a U.S. Holder’s holding period for the “new” Notes would begin on the day following the deemed exchange. The determination of the issue price of the “new” Notes for U.S. federal income tax purposes will depend upon whether a substantial amount of the “new” Notes or “old” Notes is treated as “traded on an established market” for U.S. federal income tax purposes. If a substantial amount of the “new” Notes is traded on an established market, the issue price of the “new” Notes will be an amount equal to the fair market value of the “new” Notes on the date of the deemed exchange. If a substantial amount of the “new” Notes (i) is not traded on an established market but (ii) is received in exchange for “old” Notes that are traded on an established market, the issue price of such “new” Notes will be an amount equal to the fair market value of such “old” Notes at the time of the exchange. If neither the “new” Notes nor the “old” Notes are traded on an established market, the issue price of the “new” Notes would be equal to their stated principal amount. For this purpose, the “new” Notes and “old” Notes will be treated as traded on an established market if there is a sale price or one or more firm or indicative quotes available for such Notes at any time during the 31-day period ending 15 days after the deemed exchange. Although the issue is not free from doubt, the Company expects that the “old” Notes are, and the “new” Notes will be, traded on an established market within the meaning of the applicable Treasury regulations, and that the issue price of the “new” Notes will be equal to their fair market value on the date of the deemed exchange. If the “old” Notes are deemed exchanged for “new” Notes, regardless of whether the deemed exchange were to qualify as a recapitalization, and the “issue price” of the “new” Notes (as determined above) is less than the “stated redemption price at maturity” of the “new” Notes by more than a de minimis amount, then the “new” Notes 13 would be treated as having been issued with original issue discount (“OID”) for U.S. federal income tax purposes. The “stated redemption price at maturity” of the “new” Notes is the total of all payments on the “new” Notes that are not payments of “qualified stated interest” (generally, stated interest that is unconditionally payable, in cash or property (other than debt instruments of the issuer of the notes), at least annually at a single fixed rate during the entire term of the note that appropriately takes into account the length of intervals between payments). If the “new” Notes are treated as issued with OID, a U.S. Holder of “new” Notes generally would include OID in gross income for U.S. federal income tax purposes on an annual basis under a constant yield accrual method regardless of the U.S. Holder’s regular method of accounting. As a result, the U.S. Holder may include OID in income in advance of the receipt of the cash to which such income is attributable. If, however, a U.S Holder’s adjusted tax basis in the “new” Notes immediately after the deemed exchange exceeds the issue price of the “new” Notes, the “new” Notes will be treated as having been acquired with “acquisition premium,” and the amount of OID that the U.S. Holder would include in gross income would be reduced to reflect the acquisition premium. Further, if a U.S. Holder’s adjusted tax basis in the “new” Notes immediately after the deemed exchange is greater than the stated redemption price at maturity of the “new” Notes, the U.S. Holder would not be required to include any OID in gross income for U.S. federal income tax purposes. Subject to the exceptions described in the preceding sentences, the amount of OID that would be includible in income by an initial U.S. Holder of “new” Notes would be the sum of the “daily portions” of OID with respect to the “new” Notes for each day during the taxable year or portion thereof in which the U.S. Holder holds such “new” Notes. The daily portion is determined by allocating to each day in any “accrual period” a pro-rata portion of the OID that accrued in such period. An accrual period may be of any length and may vary in length over the term of the “new” Notes, provided that each accrual period is no longer than one year and each scheduled payment of principal or interest occurs either on the first or last day of an accrual period. The amount of OID that accrues with respect to any accrual period is the excess of (i) the product of the “new” Note’s adjusted issue price at the beginning of such accrual period and its yield to maturity, determined on the basis of compounding at the close of each accrual period and properly adjusted for the length of such period, over (ii) the amount of qualified stated interest allocable to such accrual period. The “adjusted issue price” of a “new” Note at the start of any accrual period is equal to its issue price at the beginning of the previous accrual period, increased by the accrued OID for the previous accrual period and reduced by any prior payments made on such “new” Note during that period (other than payments of qualified stated interest). The stated interest paid or accrued on the “new” Notes would constitute qualified stated interest and would be taxable to a U.S. Holder as ordinary income in accordance with the U.S Holder’s method of accounting for U.S federal income tax purposes. Alternatively, a U.S. Holder may elect to include stated interest on the “new” Notes (as well as OID), and, if any, market discount, de minimis market discount and unstated interest on the “new” Notes (as adjusted by any amortizable bond premium or acquisition premium) in gross income on a constant yield basis. The mechanics and implications of such election are complex, and a U.S. Holder should consult their tax advisor regarding the advisability of making such election.
