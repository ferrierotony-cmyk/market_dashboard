If you invest in a Caliber private fund, the section titled If you invest in a Caliber fund is written for you. The basics What does Caliber do? Caliber is a real estate-focused alternative asset manager based in Scottsdale, Arizona. In plain terms: we raise money from investors into private funds that we manage. Each fund uses its own money to buy, build, and manage real estate, mostly hotels, apartments, and commercial projects in the middle market. The funds pay Caliber fees for managing that money, and Caliber earns a share of the profits when projects succeed. We have been doing this since 2009. On this page, "our funds" means the private funds Caliber manages: each fund is a separate legal company, owned by its investors, with its own money, its own loans, and its own books. What are Caliber’s two businesses? Caliber is one public company with two businesses. The first is our real estate investment platform, which has sponsored, built and managed middle-market real estate since 2009 and produces substantially all of our revenue today. The second is our tokenization platform, built around Caliber Tokenization Services, launched in August 2026, which brings tokenization to private real estate funds, starting with the funds we manage. The real estate platform is our foundation, and our objective for it is steady, consistent profitability. Tokenization is our growth engine, designed to grow through scaled technology partners rather than a large internal team. Caliber also holds LINK, the token of the Chainlink network, in its corporate treasury as part of its tokenization strategy; today it is not a pure play LINK treasury company, as explained under Tokenization platform below. How does Caliber make money? There are three primary ways Caliber makes money today: - First, management fees: the funds we manage pay Caliber a yearly fee for managing their money and assets. - Second, service fees: the funds pay Caliber when we arrange financing, oversee construction, or handle real estate transactions for them. - Third, performance fees, also called carried interest: when a project is sold and investors earn their agreed return first, Caliber receives a share of the remaining profit. Management and service fees are generally billed on a schedule; performance fees are earned only if and when projects finish and succeed. Management fees are the steady part: in the third quarter of 2026, fund management fees were $3.1 million of our $3.8 million of Platform revenue. In the future, Caliber has disclosed that it expects to generate revenue through its subsidiary, Caliber Tokenization Services, or CTS. It expects to earn one-time fees paid to tokenize a real estate offering as well as ongoing maintenance fees. These fees can be paid by Caliber’s internally managed funds as well as third-party customers. Caliber is targeting family offices with large real estate portfolios as its primary customer for CTS. Does Caliber actually collect performance fees, or are they theoretical? They are real, but they come and go with market activity. Performance allocations were meaningful in 2019, about $5 million, which was the last year Caliber operated in a “normal” real estate market (normal defined by a market not disrupted by Covid and the recent historic rise in interest rates). Sizeable performance allocations returned in 2022 for $2.5 million and about $3.7 million in 2023, tied mostly to asset sales. Understandably, performance allocations dropped to about $0.4 million in 2024 and have been minimal since, in a period when few projects were sold. This lack of sales activity is driven by the fastest increase in interest rates in approximately 40 years, starting in 2022. Performance fees can be earned only when a project sells or refinances above its investors' required returns. So far they have clustered in active markets and largely stopped in frozen ones, and whether any future sale produces one depends on that project's own results, not just on market conditions. Each quarter, the company updates its estimate of the performance allocations it could earn across the funds it manages; at September 30, 2026 that estimate was $95.9 million. It estimates the cash Caliber would receive if the assets behind it were sold in the ordinary course of business, according to each asset’s business plan. It is not money owed to Caliber; it would be earned only as assets are sold, and only if they sell at the prices their business plans assume. While this estimate does not appear on Caliber’s balance sheet, management believes it provides insight into the economic value embedded in the funds Caliber manages and into Caliber’s long-term earnings potential, which is why we update it each quarter. Who are Caliber's customers? Mostly accredited investors: individuals, families, family offices, and their financial advisors who put money into our private funds. Caliber Tokenization Services also serves family offices that own and operate significant real estate portfolios. What is the difference between investing in a Caliber fund and buying CWD stock? They are two different investments with different risks. A Caliber fund investor owns a share of specific real estate project(s) held inside that fund. A CWD stockholder owns a share of the management company that runs the funds and earns the fees. A fund can do well in a year the stock does poorly, and the other way around. The funds are separate legal companies: each fund owns its own properties and carries its own property loans, and, as described in our filings, fund assets are not pledged as collateral for CaliberCos corporate notes. Why do Caliber's financial statements include "going concern" language? Because accounting rules require it when there is substantial doubt that a company can cover what it owes as it comes due over the twelve months after its financial statements are issued. In that test, management's plans, such as refinancing debt or raising new money, count only if they are far enough along to be judged probable. As of November 13, 2026, $18.6 million of unsecured corporate and convertible notes had matured or were scheduled to mature within that period, more than the company’s cash and other liquid assets on hand. Its plans to address them include payoff agreements signed with noteholders and a financing of up to $10.0 million in stated value of senior convertible preferred stock, approved on November 10, 2026, to fund those payoffs. Even though the company has made substantial progress since it first disclosed this risk, the financing noted above has not been executed, and completing it depends on factors outside management’s control, including definitive agreements, stockholder approval and market conditions. For that reason, our Form 10-Q for the third quarter of 2026 states that the company cannot conclude it is probable its plans will be effectively implemented within that period, and that the plans do not alleviate substantial doubt. The going concern language applies to CaliberCos Inc., the management company, and it flags a liquidity condition. It is not a prediction of any particular outcome, and it does not mean the numbers are wrong. Its most recent annual financial statements received an unqualified, or clean, audit opinion, which included the going concern paragraph. It is a real risk, and addressing it is management’s top financial priority. For investors in Caliber’s funds: the funds are separate companies, and their assets are not collateral for the notes. Tokenization platform Nothing in this section is an offer to sell or a solicitation of an offer to buy any security or token. Any offering is made only through its own offering documents, and only to investors eligible under the securities laws. What is tokenization, in plain words? Today, your ownership in a private real estate fund is a paper record. This is not dissimilar to how stock used to be held with paper stock certificates. Tokenization replaces that paper with a secure digital record on a shared ledger called a blockchain. The investment itself does not change; the record-keeping does and that adds new features to your investment holding. Think of your share of a private real estate fund now as a programmable piece of software, which can further be designed to work more efficiently for you. The goals we have for tokenization are: - Lower fund operating costs - Faster, clearer reporting and valuation; improving investor confidence - Simpler transfers, where the fund’s documents and securities law allow - As securities regulation permits: o Enhanced liquidity through the introduction of the ability to borrow against your share of a fund o Enhanced liquidity through the ability to re-sell your fund shares via a digital exchange. No lending program and no market for selling a Caliber fund position exists today, and neither is assured. Tokenization does not make a bad investment good. What is Caliber Tokenization Services? A Caliber business line, announced on August 27, 2026, that provides full-service tokenization for a fee, under the Tokenized by Caliber brand. We used the capability first on funds we manage. PURE Pickleball and Padel went live with tokenized ownership on August 13, 2026, and on November 4, 2026 three more offerings followed: Caliber-Hyatt Studios Steamboat, Caliber-Hyatt Studios Phoenix/TSMC and the Caliber-HS Feeder Fund. Together the four represent up to $110 million of combined maximum offered equity, which is the most those offerings can raise, not capital already raised. We offer the service to family offices that own and operate significant real estate portfolios, and we deliver it through technology partners named in our public announcements rather than by building a large internal team. As our Form 10-Q reports, at September 30, 2026 it had not entered into client engagements or generated revenue. We do not provide revenue projections for it, and we expect to report it as its own revenue line in the future. What is CNAV? Caliber’s Automated NAV, or CNAV, is a valuation tool we are developing, described in our November 4, 2026 announcement. Maintaining regular valuations that are meaningful and simple for investors to understand has long been both a challenge and an opportunity for private funds. CNAV is designed to give investors in tokenized offerings a clearer, regular view of what their investment is worth. It is still in development, and we intend to offer it to the industry as a whole, starting with Caliber Tokenization Services clients. Can I borrow against or sell a tokenized interest? Not today. As described in our November 4, 2026 announcement, the company is developing lender rights that, together with CNAV, it believes could let investors borrow against tokenized investment interests while keeping ownership. No such lending program and no market for selling a Caliber fund position exists today, transfers remain subject to each offering’s documents and restrictions, and neither is assured. Is Caliber a LINK treasury company? No. Caliber is a real estate investment platform and a tokenization platform, and substantially all of its revenue comes from real estate. It holds LINK, the token of the Chainlink network, in its corporate treasury as a component of its tokenization strategy, which, as announced on July 2, 2026, contemplates using Chainlink’s compliance and distribution infrastructure. At September 30, 2026 that position was 229,203 LINK tokens with a fair value of $3.3 million, and our Form 10-Q describes the treasury policy under which the company holds it. Why did Caliber buy LINK? In 2025 Caliber invested $12.6 million in LINK, the token that powers the Chainlink network. The investment had two stated purposes. First, it was part of building digital asset capability into our real estate platform. Second, Nasdaq requires listed companies to keep a minimum level of stockholders’ equity, which is roughly assets minus debts. The digital asset strategy and a related preferred stock raise were intended to help the company regain compliance with that requirement, which it did. Both purposes were disclosed at the time. The LINK purchase was the birth of a new platform business for Caliber in tokenization of real estate funds. How has the LINK investment performed? It has lost money. Caliber invested $12.6 million in LINK in 2025 and held 562,535 tokens at December 31, 2025. In the first nine months of 2026 it sold 333,432 tokens for $3.0 million, well below their cost, and used the proceeds to support project-level real estate financings. At September 30, 2026 it held 229,203 LINK tokens and one ETH token, with an aggregate cost of $4.9 million and a fair value of $3.3 million, and it bought or sold none during the third quarter. At November 9, 2026, the fair value of the company’s digital assets was approximately $2.5 million. The remaining position is carried at market value, and the details are disclosed in our quarterly filings. The capability the investment seeded, tokenization, is now an operating business, described above. Is Caliber still buying LINK? Not at present; the company has paused LINK purchases. It bought its LINK with part of the proceeds of equity raises. Since then, LINK’s price has been volatile and has declined, and the capital markets have not been open to the company on terms that would support further purchases. In the first nine months of 2026 its only purchases were 100 LINK tokens, for about $1,100, and one ETH token, for about $3,000, and it bought or sold none in the third quarter. The company intends to keep LINK in its treasury and to manage it as a treasury asset: liquid, and available to be sold so the proceeds can be put to a better use when one arises. In the first nine months of 2026 it sold 333,432 tokens and used the proceeds to support project-level real estate financings, and its Form 10-Q lists selling digital assets as needed among the ways the company may address its note maturities. When capital is available and the company judges the opportunity attractive, it intends to add to the position, consistent with the treasury policy described in its Form 10-Q. This follows a global downturn in bitcoin and alternative token values which continues to persist. Caliber does not view the price decline in LINK tokens as a reflection on a decline in the value of Chainlink’s platform or services but, rather, as a function of the market for tokens overall. This page does not announce any specific purchase or sale, and any change in the position appears in our quarterly filings. Are Chainlink, Chainlink Labs, and LINK the same thing? No, and the difference matters. Chainlink is a network, meaning technology infrastructure. Chainlink Labs is a company that builds on that network. LINK is the token that powers the network, and it is what Caliber has held as a treasury investment. Real estate strategy Nothing in this section is an offer to sell or a solicitation of an offer to buy any security. Through the funds we manage: middle-market projects, mostly in growing cities. That includes hotels, apartments, build-to-rent housing, commercial projects, and land, with a specialty in Opportunity Zone projects. Middle market means real estate projects generally between $5 million and $50 million in size, too big for most local investors and too small for Wall Street. We believe that is where our local know-how matters most. The company’s long-term strategy remains focused on Hospitality, Multifamily and Industrial assets and it has taken steps over recent years to narrow its focus to these specific asset classes. Additionally, Caliber offers tax-advantaged real estate investments through its Opportunity Zone funds, its 1031 program, and its ROTH conversion program. What is an Opportunity Zone fund? Opportunity Zones are areas the federal government designated for investment by offering tax benefits to people who invest capital gains there for the long term. Caliber runs funds that build projects in these zones. Congress made the program permanent in 2025. Whether any tax benefit applies to a particular investor depends on that investor's own circumstances; we do not give tax advice. What happened to Caliber's hotels in the downturn? COVID hit hotel operations first. Then rising rates hit their loans. Our priority as manager was simple: do not sell the funds' assets into a frozen market. Acting for each fund under its fund documents, we worked with that fund's lenders and partners to hold its assets together rather than sell under pressure. In 2025 and 2026 we changed hotel management companies, first at one hotel and then at five more, and, as discussed on our second quarter 2026 earnings call, operating results at those hotels improved following the change. What is Caliber building right now? Three announced projects lead the list, each owned and financed by a Caliber-managed fund or project entity. Hyatt Studios extended-stay hotels: construction began in Steamboat Springs, Colorado on July 15, 2026, with opening expected in the second half of 2027, and a fund owns land for another near the TSMC plant in north Phoenix. And PURE Pickleball and Padel, a pickleball and padel facility under development in Scottsdale. Building takes years, and we update each project’s status in our quarterly materials. How are interest rates affecting Caliber right now? Directly. Development and financing fees are earned as projects close, and projects close when financing is available. In the middle of 2026, credit loosened and loans started getting done again. Since then, the Federal Reserve raised rates in September 2026, long-term rates rose, and lenders became more defensive, which has slowed some project timelines and is the reason the company revised its 2026 revenue guidance on November 12, 2026. Tighter credit can also bring more distressed real estate to market, as owners face loan maturities they cannot refinance. How to read our reports Why do Caliber's reports show two sets of numbers? Accounting rules sometimes require us to combine, or consolidate, the results of funds we manage into our own financial statements, even though fund investors own those assets. So, we report two views. Consolidated numbers follow GAAP, the standard accounting rules, and are the primary presentation in our filings. Platform numbers show just the management company: the fees we earn and the costs we pay. We believe Platform numbers are a useful added view of the business a CWD stockholder owns. They supplement the GAAP results, they do not replace them, and we publish the bridge between the two views every quarter. The best way to find our Platform numbers is to download the Supplemental Financial Information from our website at https://ir.caliberco.com Why did consolidated revenue fall about 60% in 2025? Two things happened at once. In 2025, three businesses whose results used to flow through our statements were deconsolidated, meaning their revenue left our income statement: Caliber Hospitality Trust, Caliber Hospitality, LP, and our Tucson Convention Center development entity. That accounting change caused most of the reported drop and it was not reflective of a drop in fees or cash generating opportunity for Caliber. Separately, Platform revenue, the operating measure, fell about 27%, from $20.9 million to $15.2 million. That part was a real decline in the business which was largely driven by one-time revenues from fees not recurring in 2025. (Source: FY2025 Form 10-K, filed 3/26/26.) What is Platform Adjusted EBITDA, and why do you highlight it? It is one measure we use to gauge whether the day-to-day management business is covering its costs. It sets aside interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, and a set of other items listed in the reconciliation, including gains and losses on our digital assets. It is a non-GAAP measure, it is not a substitute for net income or loss under GAAP, and every earnings release shows exactly how we calculate it. Caliber’s management team is focused on driving Platform Adjusted EBITDA profitability to produce positive NOI to the Company. Where does Platform revenue come from, line by line? Five lines, each reported in our quarterly supplemental: - Asset management fees, which are the recurring core; - Financing and transaction fees; - Real Estate Development fees; - Brokerage fees; and - Performance allocations. The middle three are the service fees described in How does Caliber make money. In recent years the asset management fee line has been the largest and steadiest, while development fees and performance allocations swing with project activity. The supplemental shows every line by quarter, with history. What do Managed Assets, Managed Capital, and Fair Value AUM each mean? Three different measurements we report separately. Each is a company-defined operating metric rather than a GAAP measure, and each is defined and dated in our quarterly supplemental. Managed Capital is the money investors have placed in the funds and other vehicles we manage (about $498 million at September 30, 2026). Fair Value AUM is the current value of the real estate we manage (about $737 million). Managed Assets adds the estimated cost to complete the projects our funds have underway (approximately $2.6 billion in total). What happened to the growth targets Caliber announced in 2023? In November 2023 the company set three-year targets: - $750 million of cumulative fundraising, - $3 billion of Managed Assets, and - $50 million of annualized Platform revenue by the end of 2026. On its first quarter 2025 earnings call, in May 2025, management said it was assessing whether those targets would be met by 2026 or would need to be extended. They are not the company’s current outlook and the change was primarily driven by the company’s plans to roll up 43 hotels into its hotel UPREIT being negatively affected by market conditions resulting from the increase in interest rates. In reaction to those market conditions, Caliber reset its goals, reduced operating costs significantly, and turned its attention to debt reduction and Platform profitability. For comparison, the company’s 2026 Platform revenue guidance, as revised on November 12, 2026, is $15.0 million to $17.0 million, against the $50 million revenue target.
