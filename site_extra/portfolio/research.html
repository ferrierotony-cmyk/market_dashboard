Green bond pricing research.

Fixed Income Research Analyst, UMass Lowell. May 2026 to now.

Do investors accept a lower yield on a green bond than on a conventional bond from the same issuer?

The question, the method, the finding, and what it means

The question
Is the greenium real, and is it big enough to change what an issuer should do?
Data and method
I started with 663 green bonds from Bloomberg and the Climate Bonds Initiative and narrowed them to 153 same issuer pairs under four pairing rules. Each pair is adjusted for Treasury rates, credit spreads and maturity using FRED data.
What I found
Green bonds priced 2.4 bps tighter on average. The result is not statistically significant, with a p value of 0.30, and the sample cannot distinguish 2 bps from zero. The two sovereigns that issue true twin bonds report a similar result, and Germany's March 2026 green Bund priced 1 bp through its twin.
What it means
On a $500 million 10 year deal, 2.4 bps is about $120,000 a year of coupon, which is less than the cost of the second party opinion and the reporting. On this evidence, the case for issuing a green bond rests on investor demand more than on a lower cost of funding.

Matched pairs, adjusted for rates, credit and maturity

The dataset holds 663 green bonds, of which 153 have a conventional bond from the same issuer that passes four pairing rules. The twin must be denominated in US dollars and hold the same payment rank, both bonds must be at least $250 million, and neither may be callable before 90 percent of its life. Each pair is dated at its Bloomberg pricing date and adjusted for rates, credit spreads and maturity with Treasury and Moody's data from FRED. Matches were first screened with help from Claude. Every number here is preliminary.

A small gap that the data cannot pin down

2.4 bps

The average gap by which green bonds priced tighter than their matched conventional bonds, across 153 pairs from 88 issuers. The result is not statistically significant, with a p value of 0.30, and the 95 percent interval runs from 7.1 bps tighter to 2.2 bps wider.

0 to 5 bps

The range across other estimators and sample choices. The median pair is 4.3 bps tighter and the outlier robust estimators give 3.5 to 4.5, while adding back the pairs I had excluded reduces the average to 1.2 or less. The data do not support a narrower range.

+3.7 bps

The average for the 10 pairs sold within a week of each other. The closest matches show no discount, although 10 pairs are too few to draw a firm conclusion.

p 0.67

A test of whether the gap was the same across four periods from 2016 to 2026, which found no detectable change. After the ChatGPT launch the gap was 2.5 bps smaller, with a p value of 0.61, which is not significant.

6.6 bps

The smallest true gap this sample can detect reliably. A gap of 8 bps or more is rejected, and distinguishing 5 bps from zero would require about 265 pairs. Tests for a shift over time can only detect changes of roughly 27 bps.

663 bonds

Unique green bonds after cleaning 1,226 tracker rows. They come from 291 parent issuers in 40 countries, represent about $386 billion of face value and were issued between April 2009 and May 2026. Of these, 270 have matured.

On a $500 million, 10 year deal one basis point of coupon is worth about $50,000 a year, so 2.4 bps would be worth about $120,000 a year if the gap were real. The data do not establish that it is.

There is one pattern I cannot yet explain. Pairs in which the conventional bond was issued after the green bond show a gap 11.8 bps larger than pairs in which it was issued first, with a p value of 0.008. When the twin came first the average is 3.2 bps wider, and when it came after the average is 8.6 tighter. A symmetric model should show no difference, so this needs an explanation before the headline figure is relied upon.

What the issuers with real twins see

The cleanest measurements of the greenium come from sovereigns that sell a green bond and a conventional bond with the same coupon and maturity, so that the only difference between the two is the label. Germany's March 2026 green Bund, 4 billion euros at a 3.14 percent yield, priced 1 bp through its twin, and the Finanzagentur puts the year's greenium at about half a basis point. Denmark's central bank says its recent twins trade 0 to 1 bp apart, down from 2 to 4 a few years ago, with its newest 10 year coming 1.5 to 2 bp inside.

My corporate pairs give a similar answer with more noise, which is a gap in the low single digits that is shrinking and cannot be distinguished from zero on a single deal. Bankers at Crédit Agricole cite 5 bp or more on bank paper. My financials subsample shows 5.1 bps tighter on 36 pairs, which appears consistent with that view, although a p value of 0.20 means the result is not reliable.

Two results that did not hold up

The first was a 25 bps result that turned out to be an artifact. An early version showed the gap shrinking by 25 bps, which came from measuring credit spreads over the 10 year Treasury and therefore picking up the slope of the curve. Measured over the 30 year, the credit index tracks the ICE BofA investment grade index at a correlation of 0.95, compared with 0.40 before. I withdrew the claim.

The second was a dating error in my own file. In October 2026 I checked the largest residuals on the terminal and found that the file used settlement dates where pricing dates belonged. I pulled the Bloomberg pricing dates and applied the four pairing rules my supervisor adopted, which require a US dollar twin, the same payment rank, a minimum size of $250 million for both bonds and no early call. The average moved from 4.7 to 2.4 bps and the p value from 0.11 to 0.30. The rules were set after I had seen the earlier results, and the paper discloses that.

Three things that would change these numbers

  • Verifying the largest residuals and 20 random pairs on the terminal, then rerunning with the confirmed set.
  • Replacing coupons with offering yields and daily prices, which needs TRACE and Mergent FISD access.
  • Finishing the twin search for the 164 live green bonds that still have no conventional match.
