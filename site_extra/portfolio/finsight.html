Role

Finsight.

Debt Capital Markets Analyst Intern. New York. December 2025 to September 2026.

Finsight is a fintech platform that tracks the primary bond market for bankers and investors. I was the person chasing the order books.

The job

The book chase

Syndicate desks run the order book on every new bond deal, and nobody has to tell you what is in it. I called and messaged desks at 30 plus investment grade and high yield banks for book sizes as deals moved from talk to guidance to launch, then got it out to the bankers and investors on the platform before the next update hit. Fifteen to twenty deals a day.

Once you build the relationship you get the books faster. None of those bankers owed me anything, and that was the most useful thing I learned.

A day on the desk

Seven stops, every day

High yield, then investment grade

Two desks, two different games

High yield in the spring: every deal is a story about a company, its covenants, its call structure and where the money goes. Investment grade over the summer: big frequent issuers, tight spreads, and the whole game is picking the window.

Deals I covered

The ones I still bring up

  • Alphabet's $25 billion offering, ten tranches out to 40 years, book around $115 billion. A company that had always funded itself from cash, borrowing for data centers. I chased that book all day.
  • The Paramount Skydance exchange and tender offers. Consent solicitations, exchange offers into second lien notes, and cash tenders on the 2027 and 2028 notes. I wrote the wire headlines on the liability management flow, 25 plus deals across tenders, exchanges and consents. That work is why the tender tracker and the Skydance DCF exist.
  • Relative value comps. Screens on tenor, rating and spread to gauge new issue concession, sent out with the deal terms.

The tenor screens

Where does this print sit against every comparable deal since 2022?

Ten daily screens: the lowest spreads and lowest coupons printed in the 3, 5, 7, 10 and 30 year, every investment grade deal back to 2022, by week, month and year, with issuer, ratings, size, call, final book, coupon, yield and spread on every row. A check sheet held the prints that looked wrong until I confirmed them.

When a banker asked how a new 5 year compared, the answer was one screen. Concession in practice is one deal against a thousand others, not a formula.

The end of day tally

What priced, where it came from, and how far it moved

Every afternoon the day's investment grade prints went into one table: issuer, rating, size, tenor, final book, leads, price talk, where it priced, the move from talk to print, and the identifiers. That table is where the morning wire's numbers came from.

Volume against the Street

Did the month come in where the desks said it would?

Issuance volume against the Street's estimates by day, week and month, with deal counts, tranche size, talk to print, final book, attrition, concession and end of week trading. The daily sheet went back years with holidays, zero days and Fed days marked. It is the table you open when someone says August is dead.

Backfilling the database

Checking 2019 Latin America deals one by one

The platform's history had gaps. I checked a list of 2019 Latin American deals one by one against what the platform had and sent back the missing, the wrong and the doubtful as a task list. Not glamorous. It is what makes a dataset worth paying for.

What I did on my own time

Treating it like the job, not the internship

  • Reached out to the syndicate people I was pulling books from, at BBVA, Scotiabank and HSBC, to learn how they read demand and set concession. Three are still regular conversations.
  • Taught myself the Bloomberg API in R and built the first market dashboard so I saw the curve, DV01, spreads and new issue comps every morning.
  • Flew to New York in July on my own dime to work from the office for a few days and meet the team in person.
