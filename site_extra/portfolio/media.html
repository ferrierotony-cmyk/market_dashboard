Media DCF models and credit comps.

Built October 24 and 25, 2026. Five Excel models plus a credit comps workbook. 1,860 formulas, 71 sources. Market data as of October 22 and 23, 2026.

Five media companies valued on the same basis and then compared as a credit analyst would compare them, on leverage, coverage, cash flow against debt, the equity cushion beneath the bonds and where the debt prices.

The question, the method, the finding, and what it means

The question
Valued the same way, which of five media capital structures can carry its debt, and what does each share price already assume?
Data and method
Five DCF models built from SEC filings on one template, with the same risk free rate, equity premium and cash flow definition, then a credit comparison on leverage, coverage and the equity cushion under the bonds.
What I found
Four prices reconcile with their filings within a point of growth. Comcast is priced as if cash flow shrinks 5 percent a year forever. Skydance is the only one past 3 times leverage and under 2 times coverage, and a 31 percent EBITDA miss wipes out its equity.
What it means
That cushion is why Skydance's first liens trade 170 bp wide of the BBB index while Netflix printed a 10 year at 5.346 percent in July. The 3.0 times target cannot be reached on cash flow alone, so the path runs through asset sales or equity, and I expect that to be the subject of the next transaction.

The primary market, and why a bond prices where it does

I am seeking a position in fixed income, in credit research, syndicate or debt capital markets. What interests me most is working out why a bond prices where it does, from where price talk begins to where the deal prints and how it trades the following day. The market tests that analysis within hours.

At Finsight I spent nine months as close to that process as an intern can be. I collected order books from 30 or more syndicate desks on 15 to 20 deals a day, and followed price talk to print and volume against Street estimates. I also wrote headlines on more than 25 liability management deals.

This page covers five companies I either covered or followed, valued on the same basis and then assessed as credits. Skydance is the anchor because I covered the Paramount tender and then watched the market reprice the whole capital structure when the Warner Bros. Discovery debt was issued.

A desk starts with net debt to EBITDA, then coverage, then years of free cash flow to repay the debt, then how much equity sits under the bonds if EBITDA misses. Skydance fails every test the other four pass. Net debt is 68 percent of enterprise value, free cash flow after interest is about zero in year one, and a 31 percent EBITDA miss leaves nothing for the equity.

That is why the first lien 2031s trade at 272 bp, 170 bp wide of the BBB index, and the second liens at 483 bp, while Netflix printed a 10 year at 5.346 percent in July. The spreads in the table are where I think a new 10 year prints for each name. Only the Skydance figure is anchored on sourced levels. The other four are my own inputs and are labelled as such in the workbook.

Can Skydance get to 3.0 times on cash flow alone

Management's target is 3.0 times by 2029. On my base case, with every free dollar going to debt, net leverage is 6.3 times at the end of 2027, 5.0 at the end of 2029 and 4.5 at the end of 2031. Getting to 3.0 means cutting about $47 billion of net debt on under a billion of first year free cash flow after interest. That implies asset sales or equity, which is the purpose of the second lien equity clawback.

If I covered Skydance's debt

  • Free cash flow after interest. It is about zero in year one of my base case. The first combined quarterly report on November 9 will be the first real indication of it.
  • Asset sales or equity. Getting to 3.0 times means cutting about $47 billion of net debt. The $1 billion of equity in October is 2 percent of that.
  • The first liens. They trade at 272 bp, 170 bp wide of the BBB index. With this little equity under them, I expect them to react to the stock more than a BBB bond would.
  • The other four. Netflix, Fox and Disney carry less than 2 times leverage and have the cushion to take a miss. Comcast sits in the middle at 2.3 times, and half of its capital is debt.

Five charts, one per question

The rules I held constant

  • Same discount rate inputs. 10 year Treasury 4.95 percent, equity risk premium 4.5 percent, cash tax 23 percent, market weights, Hamada relevering. Cost of debt 75 to 125 bp over Treasuries for the four investment grade names, 8 percent blended for Skydance.
  • Same cash flow definition. Adjusted EBITDA less stock compensation, cash restructuring, cash taxes on profit after tax depreciation, capex and a working capital and content line. Stock compensation is a real cost in all five.
  • Same mechanics. Mid year discounting, a first year stub, perpetuity and exit multiple side by side, three scenarios on one switch.
  • Different fiscal years. Disney and Fox run FY2027 to FY2032 with stubs of 0.94 and 0.68. The other three run calendar 2026 to 2031 with a 0.19 stub.
  • Different pending deals. Comcast is modelled before the NBCUniversal and Sky spin, Fox without Roku, and Skydance after Warner Bros. Discovery, because that deal has closed.
  • Interest expense. Comcast's and Disney's are reported. Skydance's, Netflix's and Fox's are estimates from gross debt and a blended coupon, flagged in the comps.

Betas off a quote page, and a cost of debt below the Treasury yield

The first error was treating quoted betas as unlevered betas. Netflix came out at an 11.6 percent WACC and $42 a share, and Comcast's unlevered beta fell below that of a utility. I set the betas by hand, at 0.6 for Fox, 0.7 for Comcast, 0.9 for Skydance, 1.05 for Disney and 1.2 for Netflix, and recorded the reasoning on each WACC sheet.

The second is the one a desk would notice first. I had the four investment grade names borrowing at 5 to 5.5 percent with the 10 year at 4.95, which implies 5 to 55 bp of spread, and no investment grade issuer prices at those levels. I moved them to 75 to 125 bp over Treasuries. The change has little effect on three of them and takes $3 off Comcast, because half its capital is debt.

On October 26 I checked those inputs on a terminal against where each name's long dated debt trades. Disney, Netflix and Fox were within a normal margin of error. Comcast was the exception. After three agencies placed it on negative watch for the spin, the market had it wider than my 95 bp by a margin that would not surprise a desk. Moving Comcast's cost of debt to the market level lifts its WACC to 7.8 percent and takes about $2 more off the perpetuity value. It does not change any conclusion, because the model still lands well above the price. The levels themselves are not published on this site.

What has moved since October 23

  • Skydance. On October 22 Elon Musk put about $1 billion of equity into the company, per Semafor and CNBC, and the stock was upgraded at JPMorgan on October 23 and at Citi on October 26. It closed at $8.07 on November 2 against the $7.77 in the model, inside the range on both methods. Third quarter results come November 9, the first combined print.
  • What $1 billion does. The deleveraging page above says reaching 3.0 times means cutting about $47 billion of net debt. One billion of new equity is 2 percent of that. It is the equity path the model indicated the company would need. It has begun, and it is far from complete.
  • Fox and Roku. Both sets of shareholders approved the $22 billion deal in October. The Justice Department issued a second request, so closing is now first half of 2027 at the earliest. The Fox model remains standalone, and that decision still looks appropriate.
  • Rates. The 10 year Treasury was at 5.00 percent on October 26 after 5.31 on October 5. The five models use 4.95. A move of that size within a month shows why the WACC sensitivity grids matter.
  • Supply. Investment grade issuance passed the 2020 record on October 26 at $1.76 trillion, with cloud and data center borrowers selling almost $368 billion of debt this year. That is the backdrop for the credit comps, a market that will fund almost any borrower at the right spread.
